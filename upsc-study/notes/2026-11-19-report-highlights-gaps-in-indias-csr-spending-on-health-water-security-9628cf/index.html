Report Reveals Gaps in India’s CSR Spending on Health, Water, and Innovation

A report by Sattva Consulting and India Data Insights highlights that India's Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) spending between FY23 and FY25 is heavily concentrated in a few sectors, leaving gaps in critical areas like non-communicable diseases, groundwater resilience, and innovation. This matters for UPSC aspirants as it relates to social sector governance, CSR policy, and inclusive growth.

India’s CSR spending focuses largely on education and healthcare but shows significant gaps in addressing cardiovascular diseases, groundwater resilience, and innovation.

Why is this in the news?

The report by Sattva Consulting and India Data Insights, published in November 2026, analyses CSR projects from FY23 to FY25 and identifies funding gaps in key development areas.

[1] The Hindu · Secondary

What are the main findings on CSR spending distribution?

  • Sector concentration. Nearly 80% of CSR funding is concentrated in five sectors, with education receiving 35% and healthcare 21%.
  • Gaps within health. Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) cause 60% of deaths, but 62% of NCD funding targets cancer, while cardiovascular diseases, causing over half of NCD deaths, get only 12%.
  • Education to employment gap. Youth unemployment is 9.9%, rising to 25.9% among graduates, yet only 24 paise per rupee allocated to school-to-work transition goes towards exposure to the world of work.
  • Women’s economic agency. ₹1,122.8 crore or 29% of women-focused CSR funding goes mainly to skilling and livelihoods, with barriers like inadequate care infrastructure and unsafe mobility remaining.
  • Water and innovation funding. Punjab, Rajasthan, and Haryana have 49% of critical groundwater units but receive only 7% of groundwater CSR funding; innovation receives about ₹298 crore, less than 1.2% of total CSR spending by any industry.

[1] The Hindu · Secondary

Why does this matter for social sector governance and CSR policy?

  • CSR’s role and limitations. CSR brings size and predictability to funding but cannot replace government efforts; it can be patient and risk-tolerant to test new models and scale effective solutions.
  • Need for targeted spending. Corporates must focus not just on the amount spent but on whether funds address critical problems effectively.
  • Implications for inclusive growth. Addressing gaps in health, water security, and innovation through CSR can contribute to more inclusive social development.

Back to basics: Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)

Corporate Social Responsibility is a legal requirement in India for certain companies to spend a portion of their profits on social development projects. CSR funds are used to support sectors like education, healthcare, environment, and innovation to promote inclusive growth and address social challenges.

Practice: MCQ, Mains and revision

Prelims MCQ

Consider the following statements about CSR spending in India: 1. Nearly 80% of CSR funding is concentrated in five sectors, with education and healthcare together receiving more than half of the funds. 2. Cardiovascular diseases receive the highest share of CSR funding among non-communicable diseases. Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 only
  3. Both 1 and 2
  4. Neither 1 nor 2
Answer and trap

Answer: (a). Statement 2 is incorrect because cardiovascular diseases receive only about 12% of NCD funding, while cancer receives nearly 62%.

Mains question (15 marks)

Examine the role of Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) in addressing social sector challenges in India, with reference to recent gaps identified in health, water security, and innovation funding.

Examine: Analyse how CSR contributes to social development and discuss the challenges and gaps in its current spending pattern.

Intro: Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) mandates companies to contribute to social welfare, impacting sectors like health and education.

  • Discuss the concentration of CSR funds in education and healthcare and the neglect of critical areas like cardiovascular diseases and groundwater resilience.
  • Explain the limitations of CSR in replacing government efforts and its potential to pilot innovative solutions.
  • Analyse the implications of funding gaps for inclusive growth and social equity.
  • Suggest ways to improve CSR effectiveness by targeting underfunded sectors and enhancing impact measurement.

Conclusion: CSR has potential to support inclusive development but requires strategic allocation to address critical social challenges effectively.
