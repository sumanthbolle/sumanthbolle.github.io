Media Association Challenges Digital Privacy Law in Supreme Court

The Digital News Publishers Association (DNPA) has challenged the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, arguing it violates journalistic freedom and constitutional rights. This case raises important questions on balancing privacy laws with freedom of speech and press in India.

The DNPA's Supreme Court challenge highlights conflicts between India's privacy law and press freedom under the Constitution.

Why is this in the news?

On October 27, 2026, a three-judge Bench led by Chief Justice Surya Kant is set to hear petitions including one by the DNPA challenging the Digital Personal Data Protection Act and Rules.

[1] The Hindu · Secondary

What is the nature of the challenge against the privacy law?

  • DNPA's petition. The Digital News Publishers Association contends that the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, and its 2025 Rules violate journalistic freedom by imposing restrictions that conflict with constitutional rights to information and free speech.
  • Constitutional concerns. The petition argues the law contradicts the Supreme Court's privacy judgment affirming privacy as fundamental to human dignity and life, and infringes on Article 14 and 19(1)(a) protections.
  • Impact on journalism. The law subjects publishers, editors, reporters, and correspondents to requirements like prior consent, compelled erasure, and notice, which the DNPA says threatens all journalistic activities including those by whistleblowers and RTI activists.

[1] The Hindu · Secondary

Which specific provisions are contested and why?

  • Section 36 and Rule 23. These provisions grant the Central government broad, unchecked powers to requisition information from data fiduciaries or intermediaries without judicial oversight, necessity, proportionality, or notice to affected individuals.
  • Threat to journalistic sources. The petition highlights that such powers risk identifying and targeting journalistic sources, undermining confidentiality essential to press freedom.
  • Amendments to RTI Act. Section 44(3) of the DPDP Act amends the Right to Information Act to place all categories of 'personal information' beyond disclosure, effectively barring access to information relating to public offices.

[1] The Hindu · Secondary

Why does this matter for Indian democracy and governance?

  • Freedom of press at stake. The challenge raises the issue of whether privacy laws can unduly restrict the fundamental right to freedom of speech and expression, which is vital for a functioning democracy.
  • Balancing privacy and transparency. The case tests how India balances the right to privacy with the public's right to information and the media's role in holding power accountable.
  • Implications for digital governance. The outcome will influence how digital personal data is regulated in relation to journalistic activity and the transparency of public offices under digital public infrastructure.

Back to basics: Right to Privacy and Freedom of Speech

The right to privacy is a fundamental right protecting individuals' personal information and dignity. Freedom of speech and expression allows citizens and the media to communicate and receive information freely. Balancing these rights is essential in a democracy, especially with digital data and media evolving rapidly.

Prelims MCQ

Consider the following statements about the Digital Personal Data Protection Act challenge: 1. The DNPA argues the Act violates constitutional protections of journalistic freedom. 2. Section 36 allows the government to requisition data with judicial oversight and prior notice. Which of the statements is/are correct?

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 only
  3. Both 1 and 2
  4. Neither 1 nor 2
Answer and trap

Answer: (a). Statement 2 is incorrect because Section 36 grants the government power without judicial oversight or notice.
