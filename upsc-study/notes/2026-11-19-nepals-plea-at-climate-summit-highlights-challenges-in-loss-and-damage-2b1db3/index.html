Nepal’s Climate Disaster Highlights Limits of Loss and Damage Finance

At the 2026 UN climate summit in Türkiye, Nepal appealed for urgent financial support after a devastating glacial flood caused massive loss and damage. This case exposes the gap between climate disaster impacts and the limited funds available, a key issue in international climate finance and diplomacy.

Nepal’s plea at COP31 reveals the challenges in mobilising adequate loss and damage finance for climate disaster recovery.

Why is this in the news?

Nepal’s Foreign Minister highlighted the human and economic cost of a Himalayan glacial flood at the 2026 UN climate summit in Türkiye, emphasizing the urgent need for loss and damage finance.

[1] The Hindu · Secondary

What happened in Nepal’s climate disaster and plea?

  • Glacial flood impact. On August 26, 2026, a glacial collapse in Nepal’s Himalayas caused floods that killed over 1,400 people, left more than 5,000 missing, and destroyed infrastructure including roads, bridges, and hydropower projects.
  • Economic cost. Rebuilding from the disaster is estimated to cost about one-tenth of Nepal’s entire economy, with a recovery need of $4.78 billion.
  • Appeal at COP31. Nepal’s Foreign Minister appealed at the UN climate summit for urgent financial support, highlighting the human cost and development setbacks caused by the disaster.

[1] The Hindu · Secondary

What is loss and damage finance and why does it matter?

  • Definition and purpose. Loss and damage finance helps countries cope with lasting harms from climate disasters that cannot be prevented by emission cuts, adaptation, or emergency relief.
  • Types of losses covered. It includes support for losses such as destroyed homes, livelihoods, infrastructure, displacement, and damage to ecosystems and cultural heritage.
  • Gap in funding. Despite urgent needs, available funds are far below what is required, limiting countries’ ability to recover and rebuild.

[1] The Hindu · Secondary

How is the loss and damage fund structured and performing?

  • Fund creation. The fund was established at the 2022 climate summit after years of debate, with the World Bank hosting it temporarily from 2024 and the first funding round starting in 2025.
  • Funding demand vs availability. In the first round, 119 developing countries requested $2.8 billion, but only $342 million was available, showing a large shortfall.
  • Nepal’s allocation. Nepal has been provisionally allocated $21.6 million, less than half a percent of its estimated recovery cost.
  • Challenges. The fund relies heavily on voluntary contributions, many pledged funds have not been received, and the process for accessing funds needs to be faster and simpler.

[1] The Hindu · Secondary

Why does Nepal’s plea matter for international climate cooperation?

  • Test of fund effectiveness. Nepal’s appeal tests whether the loss and damage fund can move beyond a symbolic institution to provide meaningful support.
  • Equity and responsibility. The fund recognizes that countries contributing most to greenhouse gases have greater resources and responsibility to support vulnerable nations.
  • Urgency of action. Climate disasters occur regardless of funding cycles, so timely and adequate finance is critical for affected communities to rebuild.

Back to basics: Loss and Damage Finance

Loss and damage finance refers to monetary support provided to countries affected by climate change impacts that cannot be avoided through mitigation or adaptation. It helps cover costs related to irreversible harm such as destroyed infrastructure, lost livelihoods, and displacement. This finance is distinct from emergency relief and adaptation funds, focusing on compensation for lasting damage. The concept is central to international climate negotiations, especially for vulnerable developing countries.
