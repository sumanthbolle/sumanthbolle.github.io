Ashok alleges printing of 90 lakh voter cards post-SIR; SEC refutes

R. Ashok, Leader of Opposition in Karnataka Assembly, accused the State Election Commission (SEC) of printing 90 lakh voter identity cards after the Election Commission of India's Special Intensive Revision (SIR) announcement, alleging irregularities in upcoming local polls. The SEC denied the charge, stating the printing tender was floated before the SIR and within legal bounds.

The controversy over voter card printing post-SIR highlights issues of electoral roll management and the autonomy of State Election Commissions.

Why is this in the news?

What are the allegations made by R. Ashok against the State Election Commission?

  • Printing after SIR. R. Ashok alleged that the SEC printed 90 lakh voter identity cards in Bengaluru after the Election Commission of India's Special Intensive Revision (SIR) announcement, violating norms.
  • Excess cards. He pointed out that Bengaluru's total voters after SIR were about 59 lakh, so the extra 31 lakh cards are 'bogus' and could be used for vote theft.
  • Demand for probe. Ashok accused the Congress government of involvement and demanded a Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) probe into the matter.
  • Supreme Court affidavit. He cited that the SEC had filed an affidavit in the Supreme Court stating local body elections would be held only after SIR and preparation of a new voters’ list.

[1] The Hindu · Secondary

How did the State Election Commission respond to the allegations?

  • Tender timing. The SEC Secretary, Amaresh H., denied the allegations, stating the tender for printing voter cards was floated before the SIR announcement.
  • Legal mandate. The SEC acted under Rule 26 of the Greater Bengaluru Governance (Registration of Electors) Rules, 2025, which permits printing voter identity cards.
  • SIR non-binding. The SEC clarified that the SIR by the Election Commission of India is not binding on it, as local body elections and ECI elections follow different frameworks.
  • Purpose of printing. The printing aimed to prevent impersonation, especially in densely populated urban areas.

[1] The Hindu · Secondary

Why does this controversy matter for UPSC aspirants?

  • Electoral process understanding. It highlights the distinction between the Election Commission of India and State Election Commissions in managing electoral rolls and conducting elections.
  • Constitutional bodies' autonomy. The case exemplifies issues related to the autonomy and legal mandates of constitutional and statutory electoral bodies.
  • Election integrity concerns. It raises questions on electoral roll revisions, voter identity management, and potential misuse impacting election fairness.

Back to basics: State Election Commission and Electoral Roll Revision

State Election Commissions are constitutional bodies responsible for conducting elections to local bodies like municipalities and panchayats. They operate independently of the Election Commission of India, which conducts elections for Parliament and state legislatures. Electoral roll revision is the process of updating the list of eligible voters to ensure accuracy and prevent fraud, often done through special intensive revisions or periodic updates.
