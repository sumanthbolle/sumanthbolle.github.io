Ex-CJI Gavai Alleges Bias in Judicial Appointments to President

Former Chief Justice of India B.R. Gavai wrote to President Droupadi Murmu alleging that recent judicial appointments by the Supreme Court Collegium disregard merit and inclusivity, favouring one community. This controversy raises important questions on judicial independence and the separation of powers, relevant for both Prelims and Mains.

Ex-CJI Gavai accused the Supreme Court Collegium of biased judicial appointments that undermine merit and inclusivity, highlighting tensions in India's judicial appointment process.

Why is this in the news?

On November 27, 2026, former CJI B.R. Gavai wrote to President Murmu alleging that recent Supreme Court Collegium recommendations for judicial appointments favour one community and ignore merit and inclusivity.

[1] The Hindu · Secondary

What did Ex-CJI Gavai allege about judicial appointments?

  • Allegation of bias. Justice Gavai alleged that recent Collegium recommendations show complete disregard for merit and inclusivity, favouring candidates from one particular community to the exclusion of marginalised groups and others.
  • Exclusion of marginalised groups. He highlighted that Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, Other Backward Classes, minorities, women, and even forward sections not belonging to the favoured community are being excluded.
  • Disturbing recent trend. The bias in appointments began after his tenure in the Collegium, which he said had ensured adequate representation across communities.

[1] The Hindu · Secondary

Why does this matter for judicial independence and appointments?

  • Impact on judiciary's diversity. The alleged favouritism undermines the ethos of diversity and inclusion in constitutional courts, affecting the judiciary's representative character.
  • Effect on merit and morale. Ignoring merit demoralises hardworking judges and has a chilling effect on others, including those from marginalised sections.
  • Role of government and legislature. The Union government and legislature have urged the Collegiums to include marginalised communities to restore balance, but appointments appear to ignore these requests.

[1] The Hindu · Secondary

What examples did Gavai cite to support his claims?

  • Transfers and rewards. He cited instances where a High Court Chief Justice was transferred to smaller courts and then rewarded with a prominent High Court posting due to community affiliation.
  • Punitive transfers. He mentioned a judge transferred on punitive grounds who was later rewarded with a Chief Justice position in a large High Court.
  • Recent Collegium recommendations. The letter referred to the Collegium's proposal to transfer the Tripura High Court Chief Justice to Karnataka and move the Karnataka Chief Justice to Jharkhand.

Back to basics: Judicial Appointments and Collegium System

In India, judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts are appointed through a Collegium system, where senior judges recommend candidates. This system aims to preserve judicial independence by limiting executive influence. However, it also raises issues of transparency and representation, as appointments can reflect existing networks and biases.

Practice: MCQ, Mains and revision

Prelims MCQ

Consider the following statements about the recent allegations by former CJI Gavai regarding judicial appointments: 1. He alleged that recent Collegium recommendations favour one community, ignoring merit and inclusivity. 2. The Union government has no role in judicial appointments to High Courts and the Supreme Court. Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

  1. 1 only
  2. 2 only
  3. Both 1 and 2
  4. Neither 1 nor 2
Answer and trap

Answer: (a). Statement 2 is incorrect because the Union government is an equal stakeholder in judicial appointments, as mentioned by Justice Gavai.

Mains question (15 marks)

  • Discuss the Collegium system and its role in appointments.

Conclusion: Ensuring merit and inclusivity in judicial appointments is essential to uphold the judiciary's independence and public trust.
