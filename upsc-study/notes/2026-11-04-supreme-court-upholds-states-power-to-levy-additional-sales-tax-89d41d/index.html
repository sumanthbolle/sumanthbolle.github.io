Supreme Court Confirms States’ Power to Levy Additional Sales Tax

The Supreme Court upheld a 1974 ruling affirming States' authority to impose additional sales tax as a percentage of existing sales tax, declaring a pending reference on the issue academic due to GST implementation. This ruling clarifies constitutional aspects of fiscal federalism relevant for taxation powers of States under the Constitution.

The Supreme Court reaffirmed that States can levy additional sales tax as an enhancement of sales tax rates under their constitutional powers despite GST.

Why is this in the news?

On November 4, 2026, the Supreme Court ruled that a 1974 Constitution Bench decision upholding States’ power to levy additional sales tax remains valid, but the pending reference on the issue is now academic due to the GST regime.

[1] The Hindu · Secondary

What did the Supreme Court rule about States’ power to levy additional sales tax?

  • 1974 ruling upheld. The Supreme Court unanimously held that the 1974 Constitution Bench ruling in S. Kodar vs State of Kerala, which upheld States’ power to impose additional sales tax calculated as a percentage of existing sales tax, continues to hold the field.
  • Reference declared academic. A seven-judge Bench led by CJI Surya Kant declared the pending reference on this issue academic since the challenged statutory provisions were amended and GST now governs sales tax.
  • Nature of additional tax. The Court clarified that the additional tax or surcharge is an enhancement of the sales tax rate and not a tax on dealers’ income, thus falling within States’ legislative competence under Entry 54 of List II.

[1] The Hindu · Secondary

Why does this ruling matter for the Constitution and federalism?

  • Clarifies taxation powers. The ruling confirms that States have constitutional authority to impose additional sales tax or surcharge as part of their power to tax sale or purchase of goods.
  • Distinguishes tax types. It distinguishes additional sales tax from income tax, clarifying that States cannot be restricted from levying such surcharges under their legislative domain.
  • Impact of GST. While GST subsumes many indirect taxes, this ruling affirms that the earlier constitutional interpretation on States’ powers remains valid and relevant for any residual taxation powers.

[1] The Hindu · Secondary

What was the background of the case and the legal question involved?

  • Orissa Sales Tax Act challenge. The case arose from a challenge to Section 5A of the Orissa Sales Tax Act, which imposed additional tax rates of 10% or 15% on dealers based on turnover thresholds.
  • Constitutional dispute. The question was whether the State legislature had competence under Entry 54 of List II to impose this levy or whether it was effectively an income tax, which only Parliament can impose under Entry 82 of List I.
  • Earlier Supreme Court decisions. The controversy stemmed from differing interpretations, but the 1974 five-judge Bench ruling had upheld additional sales tax as a valid enhancement of sales tax.

Back to basics: Fiscal Federalism and Taxation Powers

India’s Constitution divides taxation powers between the Union and States through three lists: Union List, State List, and Concurrent List. States have the power to tax sale or purchase of goods under Entry 54 of the State List. Additional sales tax or surcharge is a levy imposed by States as a percentage of the existing sales tax. The Goods and Services Tax (GST) is a unified tax regime that subsumes many indirect taxes, but constitutional interpretations of States’ taxation powers remain relevant for residual or specific levies.
